2015 J5 · π-day

2015 / junior / J5

官方题目PDFDMOJ

题目在问什么

用动态规划计算把 n 块派分给 k 个人的非递减分配数。每个人至少得到 1 块，前面的人不能比后面的人多。

考查知识点

  • 非递减分配：沿队伍从前往后看，每个人拿到的块数只会相同或增加。例如 [1,2,2,3] 合法；[2,1,3,2] 不合法。顺序已经由题目限制，所以不把同一组数字的重新排列算作另一种方法。
  • 动态规划：把一个大计数拆成更小计数。ways[s][p] 表示把 s 块派给恰好 p 个人的合法方法数，表格把较小的总数先算好，再用来算较大的总数。
  • 分类计数：一个分配要么至少有一个人拿 1 块，要么没有人拿 1 块。这两类互不重叠，覆盖所有分配，因此两类方法数可以相加。
  • 边界值：ways[0][0]=1 表示还没有派出任何派、也没有人时，有一种空的开始状态，方便递推。其他不可能的情况保留为 0。

一步一步想

  1. 建立 ways[s][p] 表格，先全部设为 0，再把 ways[0][0] 设为 1。
  2. 从总块数 s=1 算到 n；对每个 s，只计算 p 不超过 s 的人数，因为每人至少要有 1 块。
  3. 如果至少有一个人拿 1 块，就拿掉一个 1：剩下 s-1 块分给 p-1 个人，方法数是 ways[s-1][p-1]。
  4. 如果没有人拿 1 块，每个人至少有 2 块。先从每个人手里拿走 1 块，剩下 s-p 块仍分给 p 个人，方法数是 ways[s-p][p]。
  5. 两类相加得到 ways[s][p]。最后输出 ways[n][k]。

用例子走一遍

n=8、k=4 时，ways[8][4]=ways[7][3]+ways[4][4]=4+1=5。第一类至少有一份 1；去掉一份后，7 块给 3 人有四种：[1,1,5]、[1,2,4]、[1,3,3]、[2,2,3]。第二类没有 1；每人先拿走 1 块，4 块给 4 人只有 [1,1,1,1] 一种。加回去得到 [2,2,2,2]。合计 5 种。

为什么这样做是对的

对任意 s 和 p，合法分配恰好属于两类之一：至少有一人拿 1 块，或所有人都至少拿 2 块。第一类从递增顺序最前面去掉一个 1，会得到 s-1 块分给 p-1 人的合法非递减分配；反过来给这种分配加一个 1 并按顺序排好，就唯一还原原分配。因此第一类有 ways[s-1][p-1] 种。第二类从每个人的份额各减 1，会得到 s-p 块分给 p 人的合法非递减分配；反过来每份加 1 可唯一还原，所以第二类有 ways[s-p][p] 种。两类互不重叠且包含所有分配，故相加的递推正确。空状态 ways[0][0]=1 且其他不可能状态为 0，为递推提供正确起点；由小到大填表，因此输出的 ways[n][k] 正是所求数量。

运行时间与内存

表格最多有 (n+1)(k+1) 个数。n≤250、k≤250 时最多约 251×251 个格子；每个有效格只做一次加法，所以时间为 O(nk)，空间为 O(nk)。这远少于枚举所有分配方法。

容易出错的地方

  • 每个人至少得到 1 块，不能把 0 块的人放进分配。
  • 必须正好分给 k 个人，不是数所有人数的分配。
  • 一个分配按从前到后的份额非递减排列；不要把相同份额的重新排列重复计数。
  • 递推的第一类是 ways[s-1][p-1]，因为拿走一个 1 后少了一块也少了一个人。
  • 第二类要从 p 个人每人拿走 1 块，所以剩余总数是 s-p，不是 s-1。

Python 3 解法

solution.py
import sys


def count_ways(n, k):
    ways = [[0] * (k + 1) for _ in range(n + 1)]
    ways[0][0] = 1

    for total in range(1, n + 1):
        for people in range(1, min(total, k) + 1):
            ways[total][people] = ways[total - 1][people - 1] + ways[total - people][people]

    return ways[n][k]


if __name__ == "__main__":
    data = list(map(int, sys.stdin.buffer.read().split()))
    print(count_ways(*data))

Problem statement (English)
Problem J5: π-day
Problem Description
You may know that March 14 is known as “π-day”, since 3.14 (which is the third month and
fourteenth day) is a good approximation of π.
Mathematicians celebrate this day by eating pie.
Suppose that you have n pieces of pie, and k people who are lined up for pieces of pie. All n
pieces of pie will be given out. Each person will get at least one piece of pie, but mathematicians
are a bit greedy at times. So, they always get at least as many of pieces of pie as the person in front
of them.
For example, if you have 8 pieces of pie and 4 people in line, you could give out pieces of pie in
the following five ways (with the first person in line being the first number in the list): [1, 1, 1, 5],
[1, 1, 2, 4], [1, 1, 3, 3], [1, 2, 2, 3], [2, 2, 2, 2].
Notice that if k = n, there is only one way to give out the pieces of pie: every person gets exactly
one piece. Also, if k = 1, there is only one way to give out the pieces of pie: that single person
gets all the pieces.
Write a program that determines the number of ways that the pieces of pie can be given out.
Input Specification
The first line of input is the integer number of pieces of pie, n (1 ≤ n ≤ 250).
The second line of input is the integer k which is the number of people in line (1 ≤ k ≤ n).

For at least 20% of the marks for this problem, n ≤ 9. For at least 50% of the marks for this
problem, n ≤ 70. For at least 85% of the marks for this problem, n ≤ 120.
Output Specification
The output will consist of a single integer which is the number of ways that the pieces of pie can
be distributed. The output is guaranteed to be less that 231 .
Sample Input 1
8
4
Output for Sample Input 1
5
Sample Input 2
6
2
Output for Sample Input 2
3

Official problemPDFDMOJ

What are we asked to do?

Use dynamic programming to count non-decreasing ways to give n pieces of pie to k people. Everyone gets at least one piece, and each person gets at least as many as the person before them.

Knowledge and skills

  • A non-decreasing distribution: Along the line, each person's number of pieces stays the same or increases. [1,2,2,3] is allowed; [2,1,3,2] is not. The order is fixed by the rule, so rearranging the same numbers does not make a new way.
  • Dynamic programming: Break a large count into smaller counts. ways[s][p] means the number of allowed ways to give s pieces to exactly p people. Fill smaller totals first and use them to get larger totals.
  • Counting by cases: A distribution either has at least one person who gets 1 piece, or nobody gets 1. The cases do not overlap and cover every distribution, so their counts can be added.
  • A starting value: ways[0][0]=1 means there is one empty starting arrangement when no pieces and no people have been assigned. Other impossible situations stay at 0.

Think step by step

  1. Create the ways[s][p] table, fill it with 0, and set ways[0][0] to 1.
  2. Compute totals s from 1 to n. For each s, only compute p≤s because each person needs at least one piece.
  3. If at least one person gets 1 piece, remove one such piece. The remaining s-1 pieces go to p-1 people, giving ways[s-1][p-1] possibilities.
  4. If nobody gets 1 piece, everyone has at least 2. Take 1 piece away from each person; the remaining s-p pieces still go to p people, giving ways[s-p][p] possibilities.
  5. Add the two case counts to get ways[s][p]. Print ways[n][k].

Walk through an example

For n=8 and k=4, ways[8][4]=ways[7][3]+ways[4][4]=4+1=5. In the first case, there is at least one 1; remove one 1 and distribute 7 pieces to 3 people in four ways: [1,1,5], [1,2,4], [1,3,3], and [2,2,3]. In the second case, nobody gets 1; first take one piece from each person, leaving 4 pieces for 4 people in one way: [1,1,1,1]. Adding a piece back to each gives [2,2,2,2]. There are 5 ways total.

Why does this work?

For any s and p, every allowed distribution belongs to exactly one of two cases: at least one person gets 1 piece, or everyone gets at least 2. In the first case, remove one 1 from the beginning of the non-decreasing list. This gives an allowed distribution of s-1 pieces to p-1 people; adding one 1 to any such distribution and keeping the order restores exactly one original distribution. So this case has ways[s-1][p-1] possibilities. In the second case, subtract 1 from every person's share. This gives an allowed distribution of s-p pieces to p people; adding 1 to every share reverses the change, so this case has ways[s-p][p] possibilities. The cases are disjoint and cover all distributions, so their counts add. The empty starting state ways[0][0]=1 and zero for impossible states give the recurrence a correct base. Filling the table from smaller totals to larger ones therefore makes ways[n][k] the required count.

Time and memory

The table has at most (n+1)(k+1) entries. With n≤250 and k≤250, it has at most about 251×251 cells; each valid cell takes one addition, so time is O(nk) and space is O(nk). This is far smaller than enumerating every possible distribution.

Common mistakes

  • Everyone must receive at least 1 piece; do not allow a person to receive 0.
  • Count distributions for exactly k people, not for any number of people.
  • Shares must be non-decreasing along the line. Do not count rearrangements of the same shares as separate ways.
  • The first case uses ways[s-1][p-1] because removing a 1 removes both one piece and one person from the smaller problem.
  • The second case removes one piece from each of p people, so the remaining total is s-p, not s-1.
